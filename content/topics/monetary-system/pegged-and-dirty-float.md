title: Pegged Rate & Dirty Float
---

> Two intermediate regimes between [[fixed-vs-floating|fully fixed and fully floating]]. Teacher specifically asked *"What is a dirty float? Anybody know?"* in review — likely MC.

## Pegged exchange rate

> A country fixes its currency to a major reference currency (usually USD or EUR), so its exchange rate moves with that reference.

Popular for smaller nations:

- Imposes monetary discipline
- Lowers inflation (IMF data: pegged-rate countries average ~8% annual inflation vs 14-16% for floating regimes)

### Risk

If the country can't maintain inflation close to the anchor country's level, the peg becomes unsustainable. Speculators may attack and force devaluation.

Example: 1997 — Thailand and Malaysia abandoned dollar pegs.

### Distinction from a [[currency-board|currency board]]

Both fix the rate to an anchor currency, but:

| | Pegged rate | Currency board |
|---|---|---|
| Reserve requirement | Some, but not 100% | **At least 100%** of currency in circulation |
| Government discretion | Some (can intervene, devalue if needed) | Almost none (mechanically enforced) |
| Credibility | Lower (peg can be broken) | Higher (mechanically enforced) |
| Example | Egypt until 2016 | Hong Kong since 1983 |

## Dirty float (managed float)

> A managed float where the central bank **intervenes in the FX market** to keep the currency within an undisclosed range, despite officially being a float.

The key phrase: **"officially floats, but central bank intervenes."** Don't just say "it floats within a band" — that's too thin. The teacher wants you to mention **government intervention**.

### Example: China since 2005

China officially abandoned a hard USD peg in 2005 and announced a managed float against a basket of currencies. In practice, the People's Bank of China intervenes heavily to keep the yuan within a target range.

### Q-style answer: "What is a dirty float?"

**Answer:**

A dirty float (or managed float) is a regime where a country's currency officially floats — meaning its value is supposedly determined by market forces — but the central bank **intervenes in the foreign exchange market** to keep the currency within an undisclosed band or close to a target value.

Unlike a pure float (where the central bank lets the market set the rate without intervention), and unlike a [[currency-board|currency board]] or fixed peg (where the central bank commits explicitly to a target rate), a dirty float gives the government flexibility to manage the rate while preserving the appearance of a floating regime.

**Example:** China since 2005 — officially manages its yuan against a basket of currencies, but the People's Bank of China intervenes heavily in practice.
