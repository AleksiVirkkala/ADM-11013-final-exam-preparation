---
title: Bretton Woods System (1944-1973)
---

> Post-WWII international monetary system. Created the IMF and the World Bank. Collapsed in 1973 — context for the current floating regime.

## What it created (1944)

Conference in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire (US) in 1944 to design the post-WWII international monetary order.

| Institution / rule | What it did |
|---|---|
| **Fixed exchange rates** | Every currency pegged to USD |
| **USD pegged to gold** | \$35 per ounce of gold |
| **[[imf-vs-worldbank\|IMF]]** | Created to police the system and avoid competitive devaluations |
| **[[imf-vs-worldbank\|World Bank]]** (officially IBRD) | Created to finance post-war economic development |
| **Devaluation rules** | Currencies could devalue up to 10% without IMF approval; larger devaluations required IMF approval |

## Why it failed (1965-1973)

The Bretton Woods system "had an Achilles' heel: it could not work if the USD was under speculative attack."

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| Late 1960s | US ran inflationary monetary policy (Vietnam War + Great Society programs financed by money printing not taxes) |
| 1971 | US trade balance turned negative for the first time since 1945 |
| Aug 1971 | Speculation against the dollar overwhelmed the system. **Nixon ended dollar-gold convertibility** |
| 1973 | System formally collapsed; **floating rates emerged** |

## The Jamaica Agreement (1976)

Formalized the floating exchange rate regime. Major elements:

1. **Floating rates** accepted as permissible
2. Central banks may intervene to smooth fluctuations (managed float allowed)
3. **Gold abandoned** as a reserve asset
4. IMF members can choose their own exchange-rate regimes

## Why this still matters

The post-1973 floating regime is the world we live in. The IMF and World Bank, created by Bretton Woods, are still central institutions today.

## Could Bretton Woods come back?

Probably not, because:

- Capital flows are now vastly larger than in 1944
- Speculation killed the system once; it would do so again
- Coordinating monetary policy across many large economies is harder than ever
