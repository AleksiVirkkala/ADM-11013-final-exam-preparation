> **Q9 on practice exam.** Teacher: *"What is required to have under a currency board... should be mentioned as part of the answer. 100% reserves."* **The "100% reserves" requirement is the critical phrase.**

## Definition

> A country commits to converting its domestic currency on demand into a foreign currency at a **fixed exchange rate**. To make this credible, the currency board holds reserves of foreign currency **equal to AT LEAST 100% of the domestic currency in circulation**, at the fixed rate.

> [!danger] **MUST INCLUDE: "100% reserves" (or "at least 100%")**. Teacher said this is required for full marks on the answer.

## How it works (the mechanism)

1. The country can **only issue more domestic currency when foreign reserves back it** → can't print money to fuel inflation
2. Interest rates **adjust automatically**: if investors flee local currency, supply shrinks → rates rise → eventually it becomes attractive to hold local currency again
3. The government effectively gives up monetary policy control (interest rates are de facto set by the anchor country's central bank)

## Example: Hong Kong (since 1983)

- Pegged at **\$1 USD = HK\$7.80**
- Has held since 1983 — a remarkable run of stability
- Weathered the **1997 Asian crisis** successfully (interest rates briefly hit 20% for 3-month deposits, but the peg held)

## Counter-example: Argentina (1991-2002)

- Adopted a currency board in 1991 to fight hyperinflation
- Pegged 1 peso = 1 USD
- Worked initially — inflation collapsed
- **Failed in 2002** during economic crisis — currency board abandoned amid economic collapse
- Cause: Argentine inflation differed from US inflation; currency became overvalued and uncompetitive; the system couldn't be sustained without devaluation

## Drawbacks (also mention these for a full essay)

1. **Government loses monetary/interest policy** — interest rates de facto set by the anchor country (Hong Kong's rates effectively set by the US Fed)
2. **If local inflation > anchor country's**, currency becomes overvalued and uncompetitive
3. **Hard to abandon** without losing credibility — the Argentine collapse was traumatic
4. **Vulnerability to anchor country's policy** — if the anchor central bank makes bad decisions, the country is dragged along

## Q9 essay answer

> *"What is the purpose of a Currency Board and how does it work?"*

**Answer:**

A currency board is a monetary authority that commits a country to convert its domestic currency on demand into a designated foreign currency (the "anchor," typically USD or EUR) at a **fixed exchange rate**.

**Critical mechanism:** to make this credible, the currency board must hold **foreign-currency reserves equal to AT LEAST 100% of the domestic currency in circulation** at the fixed rate. The country can only issue more domestic currency when foreign reserves back it — preventing money-printing-driven inflation.

**Purposes:**

1. **Eliminate inflation** by removing the government's ability to print money beyond its reserves
2. **Increase credibility** with international investors and trade partners (the fixed rate is mechanically enforced, not just policy talk)
3. **Limit monetary policy abuse** — the government can't expand money supply to fund deficits

**How it works in practice:** interest rates adjust automatically — if investors lose confidence and flee the local currency, supply shrinks, rates rise, until holding local currency becomes attractive again.

**Examples:** Hong Kong (since 1983, pegged at HK\$7.80 / USD, weathered the 1997 Asian crisis); Argentina (1991-2002, ultimately abandoned during economic collapse when Argentine inflation diverged from US inflation).

**Drawbacks:** the government loses control of monetary and interest rate policy (de facto set by the anchor country); if local inflation exceeds the anchor country's, the currency becomes overvalued; abandoning the system is traumatic for credibility.
