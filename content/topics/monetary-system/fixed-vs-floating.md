## The 4 exchange-rate regimes (background)

| Regime | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Floating** | Market forces determine value | USD, EUR, GBP, JPY |
| **Pegged** | Currency fixed to a reference currency (often USD); follows the reference's movements | Egypt (until 2016), many smaller currencies |
| **Managed float / [[pegged-and-dirty-float\|Dirty float]]** | Officially floats, but central bank intervenes | China (since 2005, against a basket) |
| **Fixed** | Mutually agreed fixed rate between currencies | Pre-1973 Bretton Woods; pre-1999 European Monetary System |

## Case FOR floating rates (3 main arguments)

### 1. Monetary policy autonomy

A government can use monetary policy freely to manage its economy without worrying about maintaining a fixed exchange rate.

- High unemployment? Expand money supply → inflation rises → currency depreciates
- The depreciation offsets the inflation, so international competitiveness is preserved
- Under a fixed rate, you'd need the IMF's approval to devalue — slower and more painful

### 2. Trade balance adjustments

Persistent trade deficits cause currency to depreciate (more supply than demand). Depreciation makes exports cheaper, imports more expensive → automatically corrects the deficit.

Under fixed rates, the IMF would have to approve a devaluation — slower, requires political will.

### 3. Crisis recovery

A country hit by economic crisis sees its currency drop sharply → exports become competitive → export-led recovery.

Examples:

- **Iceland after 2008** banking crisis — krona dropped 50%, fish and aluminum exports boomed
- **South Korea after 1997** Asian crisis — won dropped 800 → 1700 per USD, export-led recovery
- **Greece couldn't do this** because it gave up its currency to adopt the euro — couldn't depreciate to recover

## Case FOR fixed rates (4 main arguments)

### 1. Monetary discipline

Maintaining a fixed rate **forces** governments to control inflation. Under floating rates, governments give in to political pressure to inflate the money supply (e.g., to fund deficits) — and then citizens pay via depreciation.

### 2. Speculation control

Floating rates are vulnerable to **destabilizing currency speculation** (bandwagon effects). Fixed rates eliminate this.

Example: rapid USD rise/fall in the 1980s was mostly driven by speculation, not fundamentals.

### 3. Reduced uncertainty for international business

Floating rates create unpredictability that hampers business planning, exporting, importing, and investing. Fixed rates reduce this uncertainty and promote international trade and investment.

### 4. The "trade balance" argument doesn't really work

Critics of floating rates note that the **40% drop in USD value between 1985-1988 did NOT correct the US trade deficit**. So the argument that floating rates self-correct via depreciation is overstated.

## So which is right?

> "Economists cannot agree." Teacher's takeaway: **be able to discuss BOTH sides intelligently. Don't take a strong stand without backing it up.**

One thing for certain: a Bretton Woods-style fixed regime probably wouldn't work today because speculation killed it then, and capital flows are now far larger.

## Q11 essay answer

> *"What are the advantages of the floating exchange rate regime versus the advantages of the fixed exchange rate regime?"*

**Answer:**

**Floating exchange rate advantages:**

1. **Monetary policy autonomy** — governments can manage the economy without defending a fixed rate. They can expand the money supply to fight unemployment, letting the currency depreciate to offset the resulting inflation.
2. **Automatic trade balance adjustments** — persistent deficits cause depreciation, which makes exports cheaper and imports more expensive, self-correcting the deficit.
3. **Crisis recovery** — a sharp depreciation can spark an export-led recovery (Iceland after 2008, South Korea after 1997). Greece couldn't do this after adopting the euro.

**Fixed exchange rate advantages:**

1. **Monetary discipline** — fixed rates force governments to control inflation; floating rates let politicians inflate to fund spending.
2. **Speculation control** — fixed rates eliminate destabilizing speculation (bandwagon effects) that drives currencies away from fundamentals.
3. **Reduced uncertainty** — fixed rates make international trade and investment more predictable, encouraging cross-border business.
4. **Counter-evidence against the trade-balance argument** — the 40% drop in USD value 1985-1988 did NOT correct the US trade deficit, so the floating-rate self-correction claim is overstated.

**Bottom line:** economists cannot agree. Floating dominates today because the 1944 Bretton Woods system collapsed under speculation and modern capital flows would amplify that risk further.
