> **Critical — the teacher drew the diagram twice in class.** Mechanism #4 of [[index|the 4 tax-reduction mechanisms]]. Q17 on the practice exam asks specifically for this. **You should be able to draw the diagram cleanly from memory.**

## Definition

> A loan between a parent and its subsidiary (or between sister subsidiaries) that is **channeled through a financial intermediary** — an international bank, usually London-based (Barclays, HSBC, etc.). The bank "fronts" for the parent.

## The diagram (memorize)

```
   Tax Haven Subsidiary (Bermuda, 0% tax)
            │
            │  Deposits $1M at 8% interest
            │  (interest received tax-free)
            ▼
       London Bank (intermediary)
            │
            │  Lends $1M at 9% interest
            │  (interest paid is tax-deductible in UK)
            ▼
   Foreign Operating Subsidiary (UK, ~50% tax)
```

## Why the bank in the middle? (must mention this for full marks)

The bank's role is to **disguise the related-party nature** of the loan:

1. A direct intra-firm loan (Bermuda → UK) would attract tax authority scrutiny
2. The host government might **reclassify** a direct loan as a **disguised dividend** → losing the tax-deductible interest treatment
3. Host governments are **less willing to block payments to international banks** (would damage country's credit reputation) than payments to a foreign parent company

So the bank provides cover AND eases capital controls.

## The economics (worked example from book p. 603)

Parent (or tax-haven sub) deposits **\$1M** in London bank at **8%** interest. Bank lends **\$1M** to UK operating subsidiary at **9%** interest.

| Flow | Amount |
|---|---:|
| UK sub pays interest to bank | \$90,000 |
| UK sub deducts interest at 50% tax → after-tax cost | **\$45,000** |
| Bank's spread (9% − 8%) | \$10,000 |
| Bank pays Bermuda 8% on \$1M | \$80,000 |
| Bermuda receives **tax-free** | **\$80,000** |

**Net effect:** \$80,000 moved from high-tax UK to zero-tax Bermuda, while the UK sub only really paid \$45,000 → **\$35,000 of "free" money** moved out of the high-tax country.

## Two purposes (must include both for full credit)

1. **Circumvent host-country capital flow restrictions** — many countries have controls on remitting funds to a foreign parent (common in countries with political risk / capital controls). The bank intermediary makes the flow less easily blocked.
2. **Tax advantages** as worked out above — interest is tax-deductible in the high-tax country; deposit interest is tax-free in the haven.

## Q17 essay answer

> *"Describe a fronting loan (préstamo subsidiado)."*

**Answer:**

A fronting loan is a loan between a parent company and a subsidiary (or between sister subsidiaries) that is **channeled through an international financial intermediary** — typically a London-based bank such as Barclays or HSBC.

**Structure:** A subsidiary in a tax haven (e.g., Bermuda, 0% income tax) deposits funds with the international bank at one interest rate. The bank then lends the same amount to a subsidiary in a high-tax country (e.g., UK, ~50% tax) at a slightly higher interest rate. The bank keeps the spread for its service.

**Worked example:** Bermuda sub deposits \$1M at 8%. London bank lends \$1M to UK sub at 9%.
- UK sub pays \$90K interest → tax-deductible at 50% → after-tax cost only \$45K
- Bank keeps \$10K spread, passes \$80K to Bermuda
- Bermuda receives \$80K tax-free
- Net: \$80K moved from UK to Bermuda; UK's real cost was \$45K → \$35K of "free" money shifted to the haven

**The bank in the middle serves two purposes:**

1. **Tax advantages** — interest is tax-deductible in the high-tax country (reducing local tax); deposit interest is tax-free in the haven.
2. **Circumventing host-country restrictions** — many countries with high political risk or capital controls block direct intra-firm transfers to a foreign parent; payments to an international bank are less easily blocked because doing so would harm the country's credit reputation. Plus, a direct intra-firm loan might be reclassified by the host government as a disguised dividend, losing the tax-deductible interest treatment.
