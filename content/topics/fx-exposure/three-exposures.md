title: 3 Types of FX Exposure (with examples)
---

> The teacher said multiple times you must **be able to provide an example for each**. Just naming them is not enough.

## 1. Transaction exposure

> The extent to which the **income from individual transactions** is affected by FX changes.

Includes obligations to buy/sell at previously agreed prices, and FX-denominated borrowing/lending. Real cash flow effect on specific deals.

### Example (book)

In 2004, a US airline orders 10 Airbus A330s at €120M each (€1.2B total), delivery 2008.

- Spot rate at signing: \$1 = €1.10 → projected cost \$1.09B
- By 2008 dollar weakened to \$1 = €0.80 → actual cost **\$1.5B**
- Transaction exposure = **\$0.41B loss**

### Other examples

- A Japanese company sells products to a US client, payment in dollars in 90 days; yen-dollar rate moves between sale and payment
- A US firm borrows yen at low rates; if yen strengthens, the firm needs more dollars to repay

## 2. Translation exposure

> The impact of FX changes on the **reported financial statements** (consolidation).

These are accounting / "paper" gains and losses — **unrealized** — but they affect the apparent leverage and equity of the parent.

### Example

US firm has a Mexican subsidiary. The peso depreciates significantly:

- Dollar value of the Mexican equity drops in consolidated books
- Consolidated balance sheet equity drops
- Debt ratio rises → cost of borrowing rises
- **No actual cash flow has happened**, but the firm's apparent financial health deteriorates

### Direct link to consolidation

This is exactly the "exchange adjustment" line in the [[../consolidation/index|consolidation exercises]]:

- Under [[../consolidation/temporal-method|Temporal]]: appears as FX gain/loss on the income statement
- Under [[../consolidation/current-rate-method|Current Rate]]: appears as Exchange Adjustment on the balance sheet (cumulative)

Translation exposure is essentially **the consolidation question viewed through a risk-management lens**.

## 3. Economic exposure

> The extent to which **future earning power** is affected by FX changes — long-run competitive position.

Not about a single transaction; about the firm's long-run pricing power and market share.

### Example

In the 1990s, the US dollar rose sharply against major currencies:

- US exporters became uncompetitive in world markets (their products priced in dollars are now expensive abroad)
- Export volumes and market share fell
- This is about the firm's positioning across years, not a specific deal

### Another example

Daimler-Benz (now Mercedes) faced rising DM (German mark) in 1990s:

- German production costs went up in dollar terms
- US-market revenue (in dollars) didn't grow proportionally
- Response: **moved production to the US** to align costs with revenue

## Reducing each exposure type

| Exposure | Tactic |
|---|---|
| **Transaction** | Financial hedges: forward contracts, currency swaps, options. Plus [[lead-lag\|leading and lagging]] |
| **Translation** | Same financial hedges: forwards, swaps, leading/lagging |
| **Economic** | **Strategic** — disperse production globally so no single currency has too much leverage on results (Toyota plants in many countries; Stanley Black & Decker's flexible-sourcing model) |

> [!important] **Economic exposure can't be hedged purely financially.** It requires **strategic / operational** decisions — moving production, sourcing, pricing, market mix. Be ready to make this distinction on the exam.

## Q-style essay answer

> *"Explain the three types of foreign-exchange exposure and give an example of each."*

**Answer:**

There are three categories of foreign-exchange exposure facing a multinational corporation:

1. **Transaction exposure** — the FX risk on individual transactions (cash flow effect). *Example:* a US airline orders Airbus aircraft priced in euros, with delivery and payment several years out. If the dollar weakens against the euro between order and payment, the dollar cost of the aircraft rises — a transaction loss.

2. **Translation exposure** — the FX effect on the firm's reported financial statements when subsidiary results are consolidated into the parent's currency. *Example:* a peso devaluation reduces the USD-translated value of a Mexican subsidiary's equity on the parent's balance sheet, even though no actual cash has moved. The firm's debt ratio rises and borrowing cost may increase.

3. **Economic exposure** — the long-run impact of FX changes on the firm's competitive position and future earning power. *Example:* a sustained appreciation of the US dollar makes US exporters less competitive globally, reducing their market share over time.

**Reduction tactics differ:** transaction and translation can be hedged with financial instruments (forward contracts, currency swaps, [[lead-lag|leading and lagging]]). Economic exposure can only be reduced **strategically** — by dispersing production globally so no single currency dominates the firm's cost or revenue base.
