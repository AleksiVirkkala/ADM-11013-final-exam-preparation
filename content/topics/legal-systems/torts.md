## Definition (memorize)

> **A tort is a wrongful act, other than a breach of contract, that results in injury to another party's person, property, dignity, or reputation, and which is recognized by statute or common law as a legitimate basis for liability.**

In plain English: **the law of civil wrongs**.

## Key distinctions

### Tort vs Criminal

|  | Tort (civil) | Criminal |
|---|---|---|
| **Parties** | Plaintiff vs Defendant | Prosecutor (state) vs Defendant |
| **Outcome** | "Liable" or "not liable" | "Guilty" or "not guilty" |
| **Standard of proof** | Preponderance of evidence (>50% likely) | Beyond a reasonable doubt |
| **Consequences** | Civil damages (defendant pays plaintiff) OR equitable relief (injunction) | Death, imprisonment, criminal fines (paid to government) |
| **Procedural rules** | Civil procedure | Criminal procedure |
| **Famous example** | OJ Simpson found **liable** in wrongful death suit (Goldman v. Simpson) | OJ Simpson found **not guilty** in murder trial (People v. Simpson) — same facts, different standards |

### Tort vs Contract

- **Contract law** governs the creation and enforcement of agreements (parties chose to bind themselves)
- **Tort law** governs default rules between parties who have NOT previously agreed to anything
- A plaintiff can combine tort and contract causes of action in a single suit (e.g., defective product → breach of warranty (contract) + negligence (tort))

### Tort = Common law (mostly) vs statutory

- Tort is primarily a **[[common-vs-civil|common law]]** subject — judge-made through thousands of court decisions
- Some areas codified into statutes; sometimes legislatures preempt the common law cause of action
- **Almost exclusively state law** in the US — each state has its own precedents
- **Restatements** (by the American Law Institute) attempt to summarize case law into rules; highly persuasive but not binding

## The 3 main types (HIGH PRIORITY — memorize each + hypothetical)

### 1. Intentional torts

> **Civil liability for harms that are caused more or less deliberately by the defendant.**

Causes of action under this umbrella:
- **Battery** — harmful or offensive contact
- **Assault** — apprehension of harmful/offensive contact (no contact required — the threat)
- **False imprisonment** — restricting freedom of movement
- **Trespass to land** — entering another's property without permission
- **Trespass to chattels** — interference with another's personal property
- **Conversion** — wrongful taking of personal property

> **Exam hypothetical:** *Person A is angry at Person B and deliberately punches them in the face → battery (intentional tort).*

**Defenses:** consent, self-defense.

### 2. Negligence (the most common type)

> **Liability for harms NOT caused deliberately, but for which the defendant was at fault because they were CARELESS in some way.**

Shorthand: **unreasonable but unintentional** interference with the plaintiff's legally protected interest.

> **Exam hypothetical:** *A driver looking at their phone runs a red light and hits a pedestrian → negligence (didn't intend to hit them, but was unreasonably careless).*
>
> Alternative hypothetical: *Person A backs up to pose for a photograph, knocking Person B into oncoming traffic → negligence.*

**Defenses:** assumption of risk, contributory negligence.

### 3. Strict liability

> **Liability where the defendant is responsible EVEN if the harm was caused by accident AND EVEN if the defendant was careful.**

This is the exception, not the norm — applies in specific situations (most famously **products liability**).

> **Exam hypothetical:** *A store sells a defectively manufactured blender. Someone buys it, it malfunctions and injures them. The store is liable under strict liability — even though the store didn't manufacture the defect and couldn't reasonably have spotted it, because the defective blender looked just like all the other blenders received from the manufacturer.*

**The idea:** the law assigns the costs of consequences of the defendant's activity to the defendant, no matter how unexpected or unavoidable.

## Memory aid: I.N.S.

- **I**ntentional torts (deliberate harm)
- **N**egligence (careless harm)
- **S**trict liability (no fault, but liable — usually products)

## Q14 essay answer

> *"What are the distinct classes of torts under Common Law? Create a hypothetical situation of tort claim under each one of the distinct types."*

**Answer:**

Tort law is the body of common law (and some codified statute) that defines civil liability for wrongful acts other than breach of contract. Plaintiffs sue defendants for damages or injunctive relief; the standard of proof is preponderance of the evidence, and the outcome is "liable" or "not liable" (in contrast to criminal cases, where the state prosecutes and the outcome is "guilty" or "not guilty" with the higher beyond-reasonable-doubt standard).

The three main types of tort under common law are:

**1. Intentional torts** — civil liability for harms caused more or less deliberately by the defendant. Causes of action include battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass, and conversion.

> *Hypothetical:* Person A is angry at Person B and deliberately punches them in the face. This is a battery (intentional tort) — A intended harmful contact, the contact occurred, and B suffered injury.

**2. Negligence** — civil liability for harms NOT caused deliberately but for which the defendant was at fault because they acted carelessly (an unreasonable but unintentional act).

> *Hypothetical:* A driver texting on their phone runs a red light and strikes a pedestrian in the crosswalk. The driver didn't intend to hit the pedestrian, but their inattention falls below the standard of reasonable care expected of motorists. The driver is liable in negligence.

**3. Strict liability** — civil liability imposed on the defendant EVEN if the harm was caused by accident AND even if the defendant exercised reasonable care. Most famously applies to products liability.

> *Hypothetical:* A retail store sells a defectively manufactured blender. The blender's flaw is not visible on inspection, and the store had no reason to suspect a defect. A customer purchases it, uses it, and is injured when it malfunctions. The store is liable under strict liability — the law assigns the cost of products-related harm to those in the chain of commerce, regardless of fault.

## Why this matters for international business

- The US has the **most extensive tort liability system** in the world → drives up cost of liability insurance for businesses
- Many people argue tort reform is needed (e.g., the McDonald's hot coffee case in New Mexico)
- Tort exposure is a real cost when entering the US market vs other markets
- [[common-vs-civil|Civil law countries]] (Mexico, France, Germany) handle equivalent issues through their codes — generally with less aggressive damages and less plaintiff-friendly procedure

## Wrongful death / survival actions

NOT a 4th type — rather a procedural mechanism for bringing a claim under one of the three types when the victim of the tort dies. Estate or relatives can sue in place of the deceased victim (wrongful death) or for what they lost (survival action).
