> Background on how contracts cross borders and on US anti-bribery law. Likely 1-2 MC questions.

## Contract law fundamentals

A **contract** = document specifying the conditions of an exchange + rights and obligations of parties.

**Contract law** = the body of law governing contract enforcement.

### How contract approach differs by legal system

- **[[common-vs-civil|Common law]]:** contracts are **very detailed** — all contingencies spelled out. Disputes are adversarial.
- **[[common-vs-civil|Civil law]]:** contracts are **short and less specific** because the code fills in the gaps.

## Cross-border contracts — whose law applies?

Two main mechanisms:

| Mechanism | What it is |
|---|---|
| **CISG** — United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods | Uniform rules for sale-of-goods contracts between firms in different countries. ~83 nations ratified as of 2016. **Applies automatically unless parties opt out**. **India and UK have NOT ratified.** |
| **Arbitration** — by the International Court of Arbitration of the International Chamber of Commerce (Paris) | When firms don't want to accept CISG. Handles 500+ requests/year from 100+ countries. |

## Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA) — likely MC

US law passed in the **1970s** after revelations of US companies bribing foreign officials.

- **Illegal to bribe a foreign government official** to win or retain business
- **Requires all publicly traded companies to keep detailed records** that would reveal violations
- Penalties: fines + criminal prosecution

### The "facilitating payments" exception

> Small payments to expedite **routine government actions** (e.g., speed up permit issuance, get vegetables off the dock) are **NOT considered bribes under FCPA**.

The distinction: facilitating payments merely speed up duties the recipient is **already obligated to perform**. Substantive bribes induce officials to do something they would not otherwise do.

This is a likely MC trap — the FCPA does NOT outlaw all foreign payments to officials.

## OECD Anti-Bribery Convention (1997)

34 major economies (most Western nations, **but NOT Russia, India, or China**) committed to making foreign-official bribery a criminal offense. Includes the same facilitating-payments exception.

## Corruption and economic growth

High corruption → reduces FDI, international trade, and growth rates.

Transparency International ranks countries:

- Cleanest: Denmark, Sweden
- Clean: US, UK, Germany
- Corrupt: Russia, India, Venezuela
- Worst: Somalia

## Property rights (related context)

**Property rights** = legal rights over the use of a resource and over the income derived from it.

### Violations of property rights

| Type | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Private action** | Theft, piracy, blackmail by private individuals (Russian Mafia, Japanese yakuza, historical US mafia) |
| **Public action** | Government officials extorting via excessive taxation, expensive licensing, asset seizure, bribery |

## Product safety and product liability (brief)

- **Product safety laws** = safety standards for products
- **Product liability** = holding firms responsible when products cause injury, death, damage (civil AND criminal)
- US has the most extensive liability laws — drives up cost of liability insurance, sometimes seen as competitive disadvantage
- Ethical issue: when home country has tougher safety standards than the foreign market, should the firm follow home standards or local relaxed ones?

This connects to [[torts|tort liability]] — products liability is a typical strict-liability tort cause of action.
