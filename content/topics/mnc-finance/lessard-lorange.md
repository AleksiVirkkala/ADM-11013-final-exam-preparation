## The problem it solves

International budgets are usually denominated in the home (corporate) currency. If exchange rates move during the year, comparing actual results against budget gets distorted — the subsidiary's "performance" gets confused with FX fluctuations.

The CFO wants to know: did the subsidiary's manager do well or badly *on the operations they control*, separately from FX movements they don't?

## The three exchange rates Lessard-Lorange identify

| Rate | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Initial rate (I)** | Spot exchange rate when the budget was *adopted* |
| **Projected rate (P)** | Forecast spot rate for end of budget period — the **forward rate**, OR a company-internal forecast called the **internal forward rate** |
| **Ending rate (E)** | Spot rate when budget vs. performance is being *compared* (at year-end) |

## The 9 combinations and the recommendation

There are 3×3 = 9 ways to translate (budget × actual). Lessard-Lorange rule out 4 as illogical, leaving 5 sensible. Of these:

> **Recommended combination: PP — use the Projected (forward) rate to translate BOTH budget AND actual.**

## Why PP?

1. **Same rate on both sides** → FX movements during the year don't distort the comparison
2. **It's forward-looking** — uses a forecast (forward rate or internal forward rate), so the comparison incorporates expected FX movements
3. Alternatives like II or EE also use "same rate on both sides", but they don't incorporate forward-looking information; PP does

## What to say on the exam

> *"What does the Lessard-Lorange model have to say about budgeting and comparison of actual results to the budget?"*

**Answer:**

The Lessard-Lorange model addresses how to translate budget figures and actual results to the parent's reporting currency when comparing subsidiary performance.

It identifies **three relevant exchange rates**:
1. **Initial (I)** — the spot rate when the budget was adopted
2. **Projected (P)** — the forward rate or company-internal forecast for end of period
3. **Ending (E)** — the actual spot rate at year-end

Of nine possible combinations of (rate for budget × rate for actual), five are sensible. **Lessard-Lorange recommend combination PP** — use the **Projected (forward) rate** to translate **both** budget AND actual.

**Why PP?** Using the same rate on both sides eliminates distortion from FX movements during the year — the subsidiary's manager is then evaluated only on operational performance (sales volumes, cost control, etc.), not on FX fluctuations they don't control. And using the forward rate (rather than initial or ending spot) ensures the comparison incorporates expected FX changes, providing the most forward-looking and realistic basis for evaluating actuals against budget.

## Why this matters for management

If you used the **Initial** rate for budget but **Ending** rate for actual:
- A subsidiary in a depreciating currency would look worse than it is (because actual is translated at a worse rate)
- A subsidiary in an appreciating currency would look better than it is
- The manager's bonus/evaluation gets distorted by FX

PP uses the same rate on both sides, killing that distortion. Plus, the projected (forward) rate already incorporates the market's expectation of FX movements.
