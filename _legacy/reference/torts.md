## Definition (memorize this for the exam)

> **A tort is a wrongful act, other than a breach of contract, that results in injury to another party's person, property, dignity, or reputation, and which is recognized by statute or common law as a legitimate basis for liability.**

In other words: **torts is the law of civil wrongs.**

---

## Key distinctions

### Tort vs Criminal

| | Tort (civil) | Criminal |
|---|---|---|
| **Parties** | Plaintiff vs Defendant | Prosecutor (state) vs Defendant |
| **Outcome** | "Liable" or "not liable" | "Guilty" or "not guilty" |
| **Standard of proof** | Preponderance of evidence (>50% likely) | Beyond a reasonable doubt |
| **Consequences** | Civil damages (defendant pays plaintiff) OR equitable relief (injunction) | Death, imprisonment, criminal fines (paid to government) |
| **Procedural rules** | Civil procedure | Criminal procedure |
| **Famous example** | OJ Simpson found **liable** in wrongful death suit (Goldman v. Simpson) | OJ Simpson found **not guilty** in murder trial (People v. Simpson) — same facts, different standard of proof, different outcomes |

### Tort vs Contract
- **Contract law** governs the creation and enforcement of *agreements* (parties chose to bind themselves)
- **Tort law** governs default rules between parties who *have not previously agreed* to anything
- A plaintiff can combine tort and contract causes of action in a single suit (e.g., defective product → breach of warranty (contract) AND negligence (tort))

### Tort = Common Law (mostly) vs Statutory Law
- Tort is primarily a **common law** subject — judge-made through thousands of court decisions
- Some areas have been **codified into statutes** (when the legislature ratifies judge-made rules, attorneys/judges look at the statute first)
- Sometimes legislatures **preempt** the common law cause of action with a statute that overrides it
- Tort law is **almost exclusively state law** (in the US), so each state has its own set of precedents

### Restatements
- **Restatement** = an attempt by the American Law Institute (ALI) to summarize case law into a coherent set of rules
- Not binding authority itself, but highly persuasive
- For torts: **Second Restatement** is the main reference; Third Restatement is partial (covers things like products liability)

## The three main types of tort liability (HIGH PRIORITY for exam)

> Practice question Q14: Create a hypothetical situation under each. Memorize the structure: definition + example.

### 1. Intentional torts
> **Definition:** Civil liability for harms that are caused **more or less deliberately** by the defendant.

**Examples of intentional tort causes of action** (umbrella term covers many):
- **Battery** — harmful or offensive contact
- **Assault** — apprehension of harmful/offensive contact (no contact required — it's the threat)
- **False imprisonment** — restricting freedom of movement
- **Trespass to land** — entering another's property without permission
- **Trespass to chattels** — interference with another's personal property
- **Conversion** — wrongful taking of personal property

**Hypothetical for the exam:** Person A is angry at Person B and deliberately punches them in the face → battery (intentional tort).

**Battery prima facie elements (what plaintiff must prove):**
1. Defendant intended to cause harmful or offensive contact
2. Harmful or offensive contact actually occurred
3. Defendant's actions caused the plaintiff harm
4. Damages

**Defenses to intentional torts** include consent and self-defense.

### 2. Negligence (the most common type)
> **Definition:** Liability for harms that are **NOT caused deliberately**, but for which the defendant **was at fault** because they were **careless** in some way.

> Shorthand: **unreasonable but unintentional** interference with the plaintiff's legally protected interest.

**Hypothetical for the exam:** A driver looking at their phone runs a red light and hits a pedestrian → negligence (didn't intend to hit them, but was unreasonably careless). OR: Person A backs up to pose for a photograph, knocking Person B into oncoming traffic → negligence.

**Defenses to negligence** include assumption of risk and contributory negligence.

### 3. Strict liability
> **Definition:** Liability where the defendant is **responsible even if the harm was caused by accident AND even if the defendant was careful.**

This is the exception, not the norm — applies in specific situations (most famously **products liability**).

**Hypothetical for the exam:** A store sells a defectively manufactured blender. Someone buys it, it malfunctions and injures them. The store is liable under strict liability — even though the store didn't manufacture the defect AND couldn't reasonably have spotted it before sale, because the defective blender looked just like all the other blenders received from the manufacturer.

**The idea:** The law assigns the costs of any consequences of the defendant's activity to the defendant, no matter how unexpected or unavoidable.

## Wrongful death / Survival actions

NOT a 4th type of tort — rather a **procedural mechanism** for bringing a claim under one of the three types when the victim of the tort dies.

Old common law had three restrictive rules (no longer apply; just background):
1. If the tortfeasor died before victim recovered → victim's right of action died too
2. If the victim died before recovering (from the tort or anything else) → the right of action died
3. If victim's death deprived family of financial support → relatives had no cause of action

**Now (modern statutes):**
- Can sue the estate of a deceased tortfeasor
- Estate or relatives can sue in place of a deceased victim using a **wrongful death** or **survival action**
- Relatives can also sue in their own right for what they lost (financial support, emotional loss)

These actions work like regular intentional tort, negligence, or strict liability claims — just with the substitution of a survivor for the original victim.

## Burden of proof and trial procedure

- **Plaintiff bears the burden of proof** for the prima facie case (for whatever claims they make)
- Plaintiff must convince the **judge** they have sufficient evidence to convince a reasonable jury
- If they can't, the judge throws the case out before it reaches the jury
- Otherwise, plaintiff must convince the **jury** to a **preponderance of the evidence** standard ("more likely than not")
- The defendant can respond by:
  1. **Disputing the prima facie case** (presenting evidence to counter plaintiff's argument)
  2. **Raising affirmative defenses** (e.g., self-defense, consent)
- Sometimes there's a **bench trial** (no jury — judge acts as fact-finder too)

## Why this matters for international business

- The US has the most extensive tort liability system in the world → drives up cost of liability insurance for businesses
- Many people argue tort reform is needed (e.g., the famous McDonald's hot coffee case)
- Tort exposure is a real cost when entering the US market vs. other markets
- Civil law countries (Mexico, France, Germany) handle equivalent issues through their codes — generally with less aggressive damages and less plaintiff-friendly procedure

> **Teacher's connection:** The teacher mentioned the McDonald's hot coffee case (Albuquerque, New Mexico) as an example of common law tort outcomes that would be very different in a civil law country like Mexico. This is a likely essay direction — be ready to compare the two legal systems' approach to civil wrongs.
