## 1. The three legal systems

| System | Basis | Where used | Key feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Common law** | Tradition, precedent, custom — judge-made law | UK, US, Canada, Australia, India, most former British colonies | Judges have power to **interpret** law for unique cases; precedents bind future courts; flexible |
| **Civil law system** | Detailed written **codes** | Germany, France, Japan, Russia, Mexico, most of continental Europe and Latin America (80+ countries) | Judges only **apply** the code; less adversarial; less flexible |
| **Theocratic law system** | Religious teachings | Most widely: Islamic law in Muslim countries (often blended with common or civil law) | Religious foundations; cannot be changed easily |

### Common law in depth
- Evolved in England over hundreds of years
- "Tradition" = a country's legal history
- "Precedent" = past court decisions binding on future cases
- "Custom" = how laws are applied in specific situations
- Judges have considerable flexibility to apply the law to unique circumstances
- Each new interpretation creates new precedent
- → contracts must be **very detailed**, spelling out all contingencies (because the law itself is "ill specified")

### Civil law in depth
- Based on detailed codes
- Judges apply codes — they don't reinterpret or create new precedents
- → contracts are **shorter and less specific** (the code already covers most issues)
- Less expensive to draft contracts; less adversarial dispute resolution

### Theocratic law (Islamic law specifically)
- Foundation: Koran + Sunnah (sayings of Prophet Muhammad) + writings of Islamic scholars
- Foundations cannot be changed (the documents are holy)
- Application is debated by jurists
- Originally **moral**, not commercial, but extends to commerce
- **Example:** prohibition of *interest* (riba) — considered usury, illegal in some Islamic states (Pakistan since 2001 requires Islamic banking methods). ~500 Islamic financial institutions worldwide manage >$1 trillion.

### Advantages and Disadvantages comparison (for Q15)

| | Common Law | Civil Law |
|---|---|---|
| **Pro** | Flexible; judges adapt to unique cases; evolves with society; protects against rigid bad outcomes | Predictable; less adversarial; cheaper contracts; clear codes; faster dispute resolution |
| **Con** | Costly contracts (must spell out everything); adversarial disputes; less predictable outcomes | Inflexible; harder to adapt to new situations not in the code; judges have less power to correct unfair outcomes |

> **For the exam essay:** The teacher dislikes the "one system is better than the other" stance — both have virtues and defects. Mention both sides honestly.

## 2. Contract law

A **contract** = document specifying the conditions of an exchange + rights and obligations of parties.

**Contract law** = the body of law governing contract enforcement.

### Difference in approach:
- **Common law:** Contracts are very detailed, all contingencies spelled out. Disputes are adversarial.
- **Civil law:** Contracts are short and less specific because the code fills in the gaps.

### When contracts cross borders: which country's law applies?
Two main mechanisms:

| Mechanism | What it is |
|---|---|
| **CISG** — United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods | Uniform set of rules for sale-of-goods contracts between firms in different countries. ~83 nations ratified as of 2016. Applies automatically unless parties opt out. India and UK have NOT ratified. |
| **Arbitration** — by the International Court of Arbitration of the International Chamber of Commerce (Paris) | When firms don't want to accept CISG. Handles 500+ requests/year from 100+ countries. |

## 3. Property rights and corruption

**Property rights** = legal rights over the use of a resource and over the income derived from it.

### Violations of property rights:

**Private action** = theft, piracy, blackmail by private individuals (e.g., "Russian Mafia" in 1990s charged "protection money"; Japanese yakuza; US mafia historically).

**Public action** = government officials (politicians, bureaucrats) extorting property via:
- Excessive taxation
- Expensive licensing/permits
- Asset seizure without compensation
- Asset redistribution
- Bribery / corruption (illegal but pervasive)

### Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA) — likely exam multiple choice

US law passed in the 1970s after revelations of US companies bribing foreign officials.
- Illegal to bribe a foreign government official to win or retain business
- Requires all publicly traded companies to keep detailed records that would reveal violations
- **Exception:** "facilitating" or "grease payments" — small payments to expedite routine government actions (e.g., speed up permit issuance, get vegetables off the dock). These are distinguishable from substantive bribes because they merely facilitate duties the recipient is already obligated to perform.

### OECD Anti-Bribery Convention (1997)
34 major economies (most Western nations, but NOT Russia, India, or China) committed to making foreign-official bribery a criminal offense. Includes the same "facilitating payments" exception.

### Corruption and economic growth
High corruption → reduces FDI, international trade, and growth rates. Transparency International ranks countries (Somalia worst, Denmark/Sweden cleanest, US/UK/Germany clean, Russia/India/Venezuela corrupt).

## 4. Intellectual Property (HIGH PRIORITY — Q12)

> "Explain the distinct classes of intellectual property" — practice question

**Intellectual property** = property that is the product of intellectual activity (software, screenplay, music, drug formula, etc.).

### The three classes (memorize these definitions):

| Class | Definition | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Patent** | Grants the inventor of a new product or process *exclusive rights* for a defined period to manufacture, use, or sell the invention | Typically 20 years (WTO TRIPS standard) |
| **Copyright** | Exclusive legal rights of authors, composers, playwrights, artists, publishers to publish and disperse their work as they see fit | 50 years after author's death (WTO TRIPS minimum) |
| **Trademark** | Designs and names, officially registered, by which merchants or manufacturers designate and differentiate their products (e.g., Christian Dior clothes, the Starbucks logo) | Indefinite while in use |

### Why IP protection matters
Without it, no incentive to invest in R&D. A pharma patent grants 20-year monopoly → recoups the $1B+ and 12-year basic research cost.

### Enforcement varies wildly by country
- China: huge piracy rates (77% software piracy in 2011, cost industry $9.8B). Famous case: Starbucks won trademark case against Shanghai Xing Ba Ke in 2006 — signaled shift in China toward IP enforcement.
- 185 countries are members of the **World Intellectual Property Organization**
- **Paris Convention for the Protection of Industrial Property** (1883) — oldest IP treaty, 170+ signatory nations
- **TRIPS** (Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights, 1994, under WTO) — minimum standards: 20-year patents, 50-year copyrights after author's death

## 5. Product safety and product liability

**Product safety laws** = safety standards for products.

**Product liability** = holding firms responsible when their products cause injury, death, or damage.

- Both **civil** (monetary damages) and **criminal** (fines, imprisonment) liability exist
- US has the most extensive liability laws — drives up cost of liability insurance, sometimes seen as a competitive disadvantage
- Ethical issue: when home country has tougher safety standards than the foreign market, should the firm follow home standards or local relaxed ones?

## 6. Political and economic systems (background, less exam-relevant)

### Political systems — two dimensions

| Dimension | Endpoints |
|---|---|
| Collectivism vs Individualism | Collectivism = society's needs primary (→ state intervention; communism). Individualism = individual freedoms primary (→ democracy, free markets) |
| Democracy vs Totalitarianism | Democracy: elected representatives, constitutional freedoms. Totalitarianism: monopolized power, freedoms denied |

**Types of totalitarianism:**
- Communist totalitarianism
- Theocratic totalitarianism
- Tribal totalitarianism
- Right-wing totalitarianism (e.g., historical fascist regimes)

### Economic systems

| System | Features |
|---|---|
| **Market economy** | Prices free, private ownership predominant |
| **Command economy** | Prices set by central planners, productive assets state-owned, private ownership forbidden |
| **Mixed economy** | Both elements |

**Privatization** = transferring state-owned assets to private ownership.

## 7. Implications for international business

The benefits, costs, and risks of doing business in a country depend on its political, economic, and legal systems.

**Most attractive country profile** (other things equal):
- Democratic political institutions
- Market-based economic system
- Strong legal system that protects property rights and limits corruption

**Reality is more nuanced:** China lacks democracy, has corruption, and shaky property rights — but Western firms invest there anyway because the market is huge and improving.
