## 1. The four exchange rate regimes

| Regime | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Floating** | Market forces determine value | USD, EUR, GBP, JPY |
| **Pegged** | Currency fixed to a reference currency (often USD); follows the reference's movements | Egypt (until 2016), many smaller currencies |
| **Managed float / Dirty float** | Officially floats, but central bank intervenes to keep value within an unofficial range | China (since 2005, against a basket of currencies) |
| **Fixed exchange rate** | Mutually agreed fixed rate between currencies | Pre-1973 Bretton Woods system; pre-1999 European Monetary System |

### Dirty float (likely exam definition question)

> A managed float where the central bank **intervenes in the FX market** to keep the currency within an undisclosed range, despite officially being a float.

Don't say just "it floats within a band" — that's too thin. The teacher wants you to mention government intervention.

## 2. The Gold Standard

### What it was

> Pegging currencies to gold and guaranteeing convertibility into gold at a fixed rate.

Adopted by most major trading nations by 1880. Each currency had a **gold par value** = the amount of currency needed to buy one ounce of gold.

Example: $1 = 23.22 grains of gold; one ounce of gold = $20.67 (480 grains/ounce). British pound = 113 grains. Therefore £1 = $20.67 / £4.25 = $4.87.

### Strength

- Self-correcting **balance-of-trade mechanism**: a country running a trade surplus accumulates gold from trading partners, money supply expands, prices rise, exports become uncompetitive, surplus shrinks. (And vice versa for deficits.)

### When did the gold standard end?

> Two key dates the teacher emphasized:

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **1914** | Most countries left gold standard at start of WWI to finance war (suspended convertibility) |
| **1919-1928** | US (1919), Great Britain (1925), France (1928) returned to gold standard |
| **1933** | Roosevelt eliminated gold standard in US (Great Depression — to expand the economy) |
| **1944** | Bretton Woods system replaced full gold standard with USD-as-anchor system |
| **1971** | Nixon ended USD convertibility into gold (the final blow to any gold linkage) |
| **1973** | Bretton Woods system formally collapsed; floating exchange rates began |

If the exam asks "when did the gold standard cease to exist?" — the practical answer is **1971** (Nixon ended dollar-gold convertibility) but the formal answer is **1973** (Bretton Woods collapse). The teacher mentioned both.

## 3. Bretton Woods (1944) and its collapse

### What it created

- **Fixed exchange rates**: every currency pegged to USD; USD pegged to gold at $35/oz
- **IMF** — to police the system and avoid competitive devaluations
- **World Bank** (formal name: International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, IBRD) — to promote post-war economic development
- Currencies could devalue up to 10% without IMF approval; larger devaluations required IMF approval

### Why it failed (1965-1973)

- US ran inflationary monetary policy in the late 1960s (Vietnam War + Great Society programs, financed by money printing not taxes)
- US trade balance turned negative in 1971 for first time since 1945
- Speculation against the dollar overwhelmed the system
- Aug 1971: Nixon ended dollar-gold convertibility
- 1973: System collapsed; floating rates emerged

The Bretton Woods system "had an Achilles' heel: it could not work if the USD was under speculative attack."

### The Jamaica Agreement (1976)

Formalized the floating exchange rate regime. Major elements:
- Floating rates accepted as permissible and central banks may intervene to smooth fluctuations
- Gold abandoned as reserve asset
- IMF members can choose own exchange-rate regime

## 4. IMF vs World Bank (HIGH PRIORITY — "must know")

| | IMF | World Bank (IBRD) |
|---|---|---|
| **Original purpose** | Maintain order in international monetary system | Finance reconstruction of war-torn Europe |
| **Today's role** | Lend to countries in financial crises (currency, banking, foreign debt); enforce macroeconomic policy conditions | Long-term loans for **economic development** in poorer countries |
| **Main activity** | Short-term loans + macro policy oversight | Long-term low-interest development loans |
| **Headquarters** | Washington, D.C. | Washington, D.C. |
| **Funding source** | Member quotas/subscriptions | Bond sales (IBRD) + member subscriptions (IDA) |
| **Two lending arms** | Standalone | IBRD (market rate to risky borrowers) + IDA (very low / 0% interest to poorest countries; 50-year repayment) |

> The teacher wants you to be able to say: **IMF maintains monetary stability; World Bank promotes development.**

### Crisis types the IMF responds to

| Crisis | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Currency crisis** | Speculative attack on a currency causes sharp depreciation OR forces government to spend reserves and raise rates to defend it. (e.g., Brazil 2002) |
| **Banking crisis** | Loss of confidence in banks → bank runs as depositors withdraw. (e.g., Iceland 2008) |
| **Foreign debt crisis** | Country can't service its foreign debt obligations. (e.g., Greece, Ireland, post-2008) |

### IMF policy conditions (controversial)

The IMF typically requires recipients to adopt **anti-inflationary monetary policy** and **reduced government spending**. Critics argue this can cause short-term contraction of demand and worsen the crisis.

### Moral hazard

The argument that IMF bailouts encourage risky behavior — "if it goes wrong, the IMF will rescue us, so why not take risks?"

## 5. Fixed vs Floating exchange rates (HIGH PRIORITY)

> Teacher: "Be prepared to talk about advantages and floating vs. fixed interest rates" — repeatedly mentioned in review

### Case FOR floating rates (3 main arguments)

**1. Monetary policy autonomy**
A government can freely use monetary policy to manage its economy without worrying about maintaining an exchange rate parity. If unemployment is high, expand money supply → inflation → currency depreciates → but no harm to international competitiveness because depreciation offsets the inflation.

**2. Trade balance adjustments**
A persistent trade deficit causes currency to depreciate (supply > demand of that currency). The depreciation makes exports cheaper and imports more expensive → corrects the deficit automatically. Under fixed rates, the IMF would have to approve a devaluation — slower and more painful.

**3. Crisis recovery**
A country hit by economic crisis sees its currency drop sharply → exports become competitive → export-led recovery. Examples: Iceland after 2008 banking crisis (krona dropped 50%, fish & aluminum exports boomed); South Korea after 1997 Asian crisis (won dropped from 800 to 1700 per USD, export-led recovery). Greece couldn't do this because it gave up its currency to adopt the euro.

### Case FOR fixed rates (4 main arguments)

**1. Monetary discipline**
Maintaining a fixed rate forces governments to control inflation. Under floating rates, governments give in to political pressure to inflate the money supply.

**2. Speculation control**
Floating rates are vulnerable to destabilizing currency speculation (bandwagon effects). Fixed rates eliminate this. Example: rapid USD rise/fall in the 1980s mostly driven by speculation, not fundamentals.

**3. Reduced uncertainty**
Floating rates create unpredictability that hampers business planning, exporting, importing, and investing. Fixed rates reduce this uncertainty and promote international trade and investment.

**4. The "trade balance" argument doesn't really work**
Critics of floating rates point out that the 40% drop in USD value between 1985-1988 did NOT correct the US trade deficit. So the argument that floating rates self-correct via depreciation is overstated.

### So which is right?

> "Economists cannot agree." Teacher's takeaway: be able to discuss BOTH sides intelligently. Don't take a strong stand without backing it up.

**One thing for certain:** A Bretton Woods-style fixed regime probably wouldn't work today because speculation killed it then and capital flows are now far larger.

## 6. Pegged exchange rates and Currency Boards

### Pegged exchange rate

> A country fixes its currency to a major reference currency (USD or EUR), so its exchange rate moves with that reference.

Popular for smaller nations. Imposes monetary discipline and tends to lower inflation. **IMF data:** countries with pegged rates have ~8% average annual inflation vs 14-16% for floating regimes.

**Risk:** if the country can't maintain its inflation rate similar to the anchor country's, the peg becomes unsustainable. Speculators may attack and force devaluation. (1997: Thailand and Malaysia abandoned dollar pegs.)

### Currency Board (HIGH PRIORITY for the exam)

> A country commits to converting its domestic currency on demand into a foreign currency at a fixed exchange rate. To make this credible, **the currency board holds reserves of foreign currency equal to AT LEAST 100% of the domestic currency in circulation**, at the fixed rate.

**EXAM-CRITICAL:** The 100% reserves requirement MUST be in your answer. Teacher said: "Should be mentioned as part of the answer."

**Mechanism:**
- Can issue more domestic currency only when foreign reserves back it → can't print money to fuel inflation
- Interest rates adjust automatically; if investors flee local currency, supply shrinks → rates rise → eventually it becomes attractive to hold local currency again

**Example:** Hong Kong (since 1983) — pegged at $1 = HK$7.80, weathered the 1997 Asian crisis successfully (interest rates briefly hit 20% for 3-month deposits, but the peg held).

**Drawbacks:**
- If local inflation > anchor country inflation, currency becomes overvalued and uncompetitive (Argentina's experience — adopted currency board in 1991, abandoned 2002 amid economic collapse)
- Government loses ability to set interest rates (de facto set by anchor country's central bank)

## 7. Currency convertibility (recap from Ch 10)

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Freely convertible** | Both residents and non-residents can convert any amount |
| **Externally convertible** | Only non-residents can; residents are restricted |
| **Nonconvertible** | Neither can — historical example: USSR |

**Capital flight** = mass exodus of money from a currency, leading to exchange rate collapse.

## 8. Implications for international business (managerial)

### Currency management
The current system is volatile; companies in significant FX activity need to use forward contracts, swaps, and other instruments. Use of these has surged since Bretton Woods collapsed.

### Business strategy
Volatile exchange rates are difficult to predict but can have major competitive impact. Strategies to reduce **economic exposure**:
- Disperse production globally (Daimler-Benz moved production to US in 1990s when DM strengthened; Toyota factories worldwide; Airbus shifted suppliers to USD-denominated suppliers)
- Use forward markets for short-term hedging
- Contracting out manufacturing (works for low-value-added; risky for high-value-added)

### Corporate-government relations
Businesses can lobby governments on monetary policy. They generally benefit from stability — a regime that minimizes volatility (especially volatility unrelated to fundamentals) is best for international business.
